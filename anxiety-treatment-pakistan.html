The Kinds of Anxiety We Treat

"Anxiety" is not one thing. Naming yours is the first step to treating it.

Generalized Anxiety (GAD)

Chronic worry that jumps from money to health to family and back — a mind that never switches off, and a body that stays braced for bad news.

Panic Attacks

Sudden waves of pounding heart, breathlessness, and the terror that you are dying or losing control. CBT breaks the fear-of-fear cycle that keeps panic returning.

Social Anxiety

Dreading gatherings, interviews, or being judged — exhausting in a society where dawats, weddings, and log kya kahenge are unavoidable.

Health Anxiety

Every headache is a tumour, every flutter a heart attack. Repeated tests come back clear, yet the fear does not leave. This is treatable.

Severe & Chronic Anxiety

Anxiety that has lasted years and now feels like personality. It is not — long-standing anxiety still responds to structured treatment.

When Anxiety Lives in the Body

In Pakistan, anxiety very often arrives at a cardiologist's clinic before it ever reaches a therapist. Palpitations, chest tightness, a churning stomach, trembling, dizziness — "ghabrahat" — get investigated as heart or stomach problems. The tests come back normal, a tonic is prescribed, and the person leaves without the one diagnosis that would actually explain everything.

If you have had the ECGs and the endoscopies and been told "everything is fine" while feeling anything but fine — that pattern itself is one of the most recognizable signatures of an anxiety disorder. Your symptoms are real. They are physical. And they are produced by a nervous system stuck in alarm mode, which is precisely what CBT retrains.

A systematic review published in the BMJ found anxiety and depressive disorders affecting around a third of Pakistanis, with socioeconomic pressure and relationship problems as the biggest drivers — and most sufferers never receiving treatment. You do not have to be one of them.

How Anxiety Treatment Works

CBT is structured and practical — you leave every session with something to use.

1. Assessment

We map your anxiety: what triggers it, how it shows up in your body, what you avoid because of it, and what it is costing you. You set the pace.

2. Retraining the Thoughts

CBT — the most consistently supported treatment for anxiety disorders — teaches you to catch catastrophic predictions and test them against reality, instead of obeying them.

3. Reclaiming What You Avoid

Avoidance shrinks life and feeds anxiety. Gradually — never forced — you re-enter the situations anxiety took from you, and discover you can handle them.

4. Drug-Free First, Honest Always

CBT is a medication-free treatment, and for most anxiety it is enough. If yours is severe enough that a psychiatric consult would help, I will say so and help you find one. Psychiatrist vs psychologist →

What CBT for Anxiety Looks Like in a Session with Attia

Sessions are 50 minutes on Zoom, in Urdu, English or Punjabi. A typical course looks like this:

  1. Sessions 1–2: getting to know your anxiety. When it started, what sets it off, and how it shows up in your body, your thoughts and what you do. Together we agree what you want to change.
  2. Sessions 3–6: the core skills. You learn to catch the thoughts that drive the fear and test them against what actually happens, and you build a small toolkit for the physical side: breathing and grounding.
  3. Sessions 7–12: facing what you avoid. Step by step, at a pace you agree to, you go back into the situations anxiety has taken from you.
  4. The last sessions: keeping it. We plan for setbacks, so the progress holds after therapy ends.

There are short exercises to do between sessions, a few minutes a day. Some people need fewer sessions; anxiety you have lived with for years may need more. We review it together as we go.

The thinking traps that come up most often:

  • Catastrophising: "If this goes wrong, everything is finished."
  • Mind-reading: "Everyone thinks I'm stupid."
  • Overestimating danger: "Something bad is definitely going to happen."
  • All-or-nothing thinking: "If I'm not perfect, I've failed."

"Log Kya Kahenge": The Labels, and What Anxiety Sounds Like at Home

Plenty of people hide anxiety for years because of the labels that come with it: kamzor eman (weak faith), nafsiyati (mental), or simply "drama". Many of Attia's clients say they kept their symptoms from family because they were afraid of bringing shame on them. Anxiety is a health condition, like high blood pressure, and it responds to treatment.

At home it rarely sounds like "I'm anxious". More often it sounds like:

  • a gola in the throat, or a stomach that is never settled;
  • back pain, headaches or tight shoulders that tests can't explain;
  • constant worry that something will happen to your parents, your spouse or your children;
  • finding reasons to miss weddings and dawats so no one can judge you.

Women carry more of it. Across the Pakistani community studies in the BMJ review, rates of anxiety and depression ranged from 29% to 66% in women, against 10% to 33% in men.

When the Worry Is About Money

"I wake up at night thinking about the electricity bill." Money anxiety is different from other worry because the threat is real. The trouble is that a mind stuck in survival mode gets worse, not better, at solving the problem.

Money and mental health are closely tied. A review of 65 studies found that people in debt were much more likely to have depression and other mental health problems, although it could not say which comes first.

Two things that help:

  • Shrink your focus to what you control. You can't control the dollar rate or the price of petrol. You can control how the stress lands on the people at home, what you cut back on, and whether you still sleep and walk.
  • Give the worry a window. Pick a set half hour, say 5:00 to 5:30pm, for bills and budgets. When it ends, close the notebook and tell yourself: "I've done today's work. Worrying more won't add money."

Many men were raised to see their worth only as providers. For them, money pressure often comes out as irritability or silence rather than worry. If that sounds like someone at home, therapy can help them too.

Work Stress and Burnout

The boss on WhatsApp at 10pm. A client who wants the revision on Sunday. A job title that says "designer" but means designer, admin and social media.

The World Health Organization describes burnout as the result of workplace stress that hasn't been managed, with three parts: exhaustion, growing distance or cynicism about your job, and feeling less effective at it. It is classed as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition, and it overlaps with anxiety and depression, which is one reason it helps to have someone look at the whole picture with you.

For many working women there is a second shift at home: cooking, childcare and family duties after a full day's work.

Leaving your job isn't always an option. What can help in the meantime:

  • Say your hours. Answer one 11pm email and it becomes the expectation. "I'll look at this first thing tomorrow" is a complete sentence.
  • Make a gap between work and home. Change your clothes or take a 15-minute walk before you walk in, even if "work" was the next room.
  • Practise saying no. In sessions we work on setting limits at work without dreading the conflict.

For students, see exam stress during MDCAT and CAIEs.

If you are in crisis: if you are having thoughts of harming yourself, please don't wait for a therapy appointment. Go to your nearest hospital emergency department, call Rescue 1122 or the police on 15, or tell someone you trust right now. Outside Pakistan, call your local emergency number.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is anxiety treated?

The most researched and effective treatment is CBT. It teaches you to identify the thought patterns fueling your anxiety, change your relationship with the physical symptoms, and gradually take back the situations you avoid. Severe anxiety sometimes benefits from medication alongside therapy, via a psychiatrist.

Can anxiety be treated without medication?

Yes. CBT is a drug-free, evidence-based treatment and a recommended first-line option for most anxiety disorders. Many people recover with therapy alone; if your anxiety is severe, a combination may work best — and you will get an honest recommendation either way.

How much does anxiety treatment cost in Pakistan?

Sessions are PKR 5,000 for a 50-minute online session — the same flat rate as every other session type. No assessment fees or hidden charges.

Do you treat panic attacks?

Yes. Panic responds very well to CBT. You learn what a panic attack actually is physiologically, why it cannot harm you, and the specific techniques that break the fear-of-fear cycle that keeps panic returning.

Can I get anxiety therapy in Urdu?

How many sessions will I need?

Many clients notice meaningful change within 6–12 sessions; the APA reports about half of people recover within 15–20 sessions. You are never locked into a package.
