Hurt by Your Husband, Wife or In-Laws? How to Begin Healing

By Attia Altaf · Healing with Attia

UK-qualified (CPCAB) Integrative Psychotherapist, 8+ years' experience.

Published Jun 25, 2026 · Updated Nov 24, 2026

The taunt at the dinner table. Being compared, in front of guests, to someone else's daughter-in-law. Decisions about your own home made without you. A husband who goes quiet when his mother is in the room, or a wife who hasn't spoken to you properly in weeks. And at the end of it, the advice: "adjust karo".

Hurt from a stranger fades. Hurt from your spouse or your in-laws is different, because you go home to it. This page is about that kind of hurt: what helps, and when marriage counseling is worth it.

Why Hurt at Home Cuts So Deep

You can't simply walk away from family. You share a kitchen, children, and often a roof. The person you would normally turn to for comfort may be the one who hurt you, or the one who stayed silent while it happened. And izzat makes it hard to tell anyone outside.

It also affects your health. In a small study of 73 married women at a hospital clinic in Ghizar, Gilgit-Baltistan, those who described their relations with their in-laws as not cordial were around three and a half times more likely to be depressed. It was a pilot study, but the pattern will be familiar to many families.

What Helps

1. Let yourself feel it

Anger, grief and confusion are normal, not weakness. Pretending you're fine doesn't make it heal faster.

2. Set limits, even inside the family

You have the right to protect yourself. You might stop discussing your marriage in front of relatives, leave the room when a conversation turns into taunts, or ask that some decisions be made by the two of you. For more on this, see living in a joint family without losing yourself.

3. Talk to your spouse about the hurt, not about their family

"Your mother is impossible" starts a fight about loyalty. "When I'm corrected in front of guests, I feel small, and I need you to say something" is about you and what you need, and it is much harder to argue with.

4. Move from "why" to "what now"

"Why do they treat me like this?" keeps you inside their story. "What can I change about how I respond, starting this week?" gives some of the power back to you.

5. Lean on people who are safe

Choose people who listen, not the ones who say "at least he doesn't…" or "be grateful".

6. Forgive for your own peace, if and when you're ready

Forgiveness doesn't mean the behaviour was acceptable, and it doesn't require reconciliation. In a 2024 trial with 4,598 people in five countries, a two-week self-help forgiveness workbook led to small but real drops in anxiety and depression. You can forgive and still keep firm boundaries.

What a First Marriage-Counseling Session Does With This Kind of Hurt

The first session is about understanding your relationship: how it began, what has changed, what you need, and what you have already tried. Attia listens without judgement before making any assessment.

After that, sessions look for the real pattern underneath the arguments. Often it is a cycle: one person pushes, the other withdraws, and the in-laws are part of the system. You work on your part of it, because that is the part you can change: raising issues without blame, holding boundaries with in-laws calmly, and responding instead of reacting. Sessions are 50 minutes, online, in Urdu, English or Punjabi.

You Can Start Alone

You don't need your spouse to agree before you begin. Counseling at Healing with Attia is individual by default: you attend on your own, work through what is happening in your marriage, and use what you learn at home. Working alone is not the consolation prize. When one partner is dismissive of therapy, or emotions run too hot for joint conversations, working on your side of the marriage is the sensible step. Sessions are always one-to-one.

If you are not safe: if you are being hit, threatened, locked in or controlled, your safety comes before counseling. In Pakistan, call the police on 15 or the Madadgaar National Helpline on 1098 for women and children facing violence, or go to someone you trust.

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, go to your nearest hospital emergency department, call Rescue 1122, or tell someone you trust right now. Outside Pakistan, call your local emergency number.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get marriage counseling if my husband or wife won't come?

Yes. Sessions at Healing with Attia are individual by default. A marriage is a system: when one person changes how they communicate, hold boundaries and respond to conflict, the dynamic of the whole marriage can shift. Sessions are always one-to-one: your spouse never joins, and they never need to know.

Is it okay to limit contact with in-laws who hurt me?

Yes. Protecting your wellbeing is not disloyalty. Where cutting ties isn't possible, firm limits are a healthy way to stay safe while you heal: not discussing your marriage in front of relatives, leaving conversations that turn into taunts, and asking your spouse to deal with their own family.

Should I forgive my spouse or in-laws?

Forgiveness is for your peace, not their excuse, and it does not require you to accept the behaviour or reconcile. You can forgive on your own timeline and still keep firm boundaries. It cannot be forced.

How long does it take to heal from this kind of hurt?

There's no fixed timeline. It depends on how deep the hurt is, whether it is still happening, and what support you have. When you still live with the people who hurt you, healing usually goes alongside changing how you deal with them, which is where counseling helps.
