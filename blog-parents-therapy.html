"Log Kya Kahenge?" How to Talk to Your Parents About Therapy

By Attia Altaf · Healing with Attia

UK-qualified (CPCAB) Integrative Psychotherapist, 8+ years' experience.

Published Feb 9, 2026 · Updated Jul 7, 2026

"You have a roof over your head and food on the table. What do you have to be depressed about?"

If you have tried to tell your parents you're struggling, you may have heard this, or "bas namaz parha karo" (just pray more). It can feel like a wall. Usually it isn't a wall. It's fear, and fear can be talked to.

What Your Parents Are Actually Worried About

Before the conversation, it helps to know what is going on for them. In a desi family the worries tend to be these:

  • Izzat and log kya kahenge. "People will think we failed as parents." In a family, one person's struggle can feel like everyone's shame.
  • Rishtay. "Who will marry you if this gets out?" This fear is real and common. In a 2024 study of young Pakistani women and their parents, one parent put it plainly: if a daughter gets labelled as having mental health issues, "she's definitely not getting married". The researchers found stigma was the main thing standing between these families and help.
  • Faith. "If you're sad, your connection with Allah must be weak."
  • Blame. "Is this our fault?" Many parents hear "I need therapy" as "you did something wrong".
  • The stranger. "What if the therapist turns you against us?"

None of these is a reason to give up. Each one has an answer, and the answers are below.

How to Open the Conversation

  • Pick the moment. Not in the middle of an argument, not in front of relatives. A quiet evening, or a drive, works better than a family dinner.
  • Start with one parent, or an aunt, uncle or older cousin who is likely to understand and can help you with the other.
  • Describe what's happening, not a diagnosis. "I can't sleep, I can't concentrate, and I feel heavy most days" lands more gently than "I have depression".
  • Make it small. Ask for a few sessions, then a review together, not a promise of years.
  • Say clearly that it isn't about blame. Then give them time. A first "no" is often a "let me think".

A short script

Change the words to fit your family. Saying it in the language you speak at home usually goes further than saying it in English.

Urdu

"Ammi, Abbu, mujhe aap se ek zaroori baat karni hai. Pehle bas sun lijiye. Kuch mahinon se meri neend theek nahi, kisi kaam mein dil nahi lagta, aur zyada tar din bojh sa mehsoos hota hai. Maine khud sambhalne ki bohat koshish ki hai. Main ek trained counsellor se kuch dafa online baat karna chahti/chahta hoon. Yeh bilkul private hoga. Yeh aap ki kisi galti ki wajah se nahi hai. Jaise kamar mein dard ho to doctor ko dikhate hain, waise hi. Kya hum chaar sessions try kar ke phir dobara baat kar sakte hain?"

English

"Ammi, Abbu, I need to talk to you about something important. Please just listen first. For a few months I haven't been sleeping properly, nothing feels interesting, and most days feel heavy. I've tried hard to handle it on my own. I'd like to talk to a trained counsellor a few times, online. It will be completely private. It isn't because of anything you did. It's like seeing a doctor when your back hurts. Can we try four sessions and then talk again?"

If they push back

  • "Log kya kahenge?" Nobody needs to know. Sessions are online, from your own room, and confidential.
  • "Just pray more." You can do both. The Prophet (PBUH) was asked whether to tie a camel or leave it loose and trust in Allah. He said: "Tie it and rely upon Allah". Therapy is tying the camel.
  • "Is this our fault?" "No. I want to feel better, and I want things at home to be calmer, not to blame anyone."
  • "The therapist will turn you against us." The point of therapy is to understand yourself and handle things better, including at home. It isn't to take sides.
  • "It's too expensive." Fees are on the services page. Agreeing a few sessions first keeps the cost clear.

If you are an adult, you don't need anyone's permission to see a therapist, and nothing you say in sessions is shared with your family. Telling them is your choice, and it can come later.

Living Abroad, Talking to Parents Back Home

If you live in the UK, the Gulf or further away, the conversation happens on a phone screen, often across a time difference, and your parents may hear "therapy" as something foreign. The same 2024 study, with Pakistani families in England, found that the pull between two cultures made it harder for both young women and their parents to look for help.

  • Call at a calm time in their day, not late at night in Pakistan.
  • Say that the counsellor is Pakistani and that sessions can be in Urdu or Punjabi. To many parents that sounds less foreign than "a therapist".
  • Keep it about how you are, not about the past.

Sessions with Attia are online in Urdu, English and Punjabi, at times that work from abroad: see online therapy for Pakistanis in the UK, in the UAE, in Saudi Arabia, or anywhere else abroad.

If you are in crisis: if you are having thoughts of harming yourself, please don't wait for your parents to agree to anything. In Pakistan, go to your nearest hospital emergency department, call Rescue 1122 or the police on 15, or tell someone you trust right now. Outside Pakistan, call your local emergency number.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a mental health professional and how do they help?

A mental health professional is a trained specialist who helps people manage psychological difficulties, including depression, anxiety, trauma, and relationship issues. In Pakistan, the main types are: Psychotherapists/Counselors (like Attia Altaf), who provide talk therapy; Clinical Psychologists, who assess and treat mental health conditions; and Psychiatrists, who prescribe medication. A psychotherapist is the right first step for most people dealing with stress, anxiety, or relationship problems.

Is online therapy a good option for depression in Pakistan?

Yes. Therapy for depression is available online across Pakistan at Healing with Attia. Evidence-based approaches like CBT and Integrative Therapy are highly effective for depression and can be delivered fully online via Zoom. Sessions are confidential, culturally sensitive, and conducted in Urdu or English. You do not need a referral to begin.

Where should I start looking for a mental health professional in Pakistan?

Finding a mental health professional in Pakistan can feel overwhelming due to limited public resources and social stigma. The easiest way to start is by booking a WhatsApp consultation with Healing with Attia — there is no waiting list, sessions are online (so no commute), and the first step is simply a conversation about what you are struggling with.

What if my parents find out I am seeing a mental health professional?

Confidentiality is a core principle of therapy. Your therapist will never share your session content with your parents or anyone else without your consent. If you are an adult (18+), your sessions are entirely private. If you are a teenager and concerned about privacy, discuss this with your therapist at the first session so appropriate boundaries can be established.
