Mental Health in Pakistan: The Mind Matters Conversation

Integrative Psychotherapist Attia Altaf joins Saad Zulfiqar Khan to talk about what mental illness actually is, how stress quietly turns into anxiety and then depression, why so many people here suffer in silence, and what parents get wrong without meaning to.

What Is Mental Illness, Exactly?

Attia Altaf01:14

Okay. Mental health illness can be looked at from many different angles. But basically, the way I understand it is this: it's your basic motivation to live a meaningful life. And within that, your emotions, your feelings and your motivation are aligned with some goal that you want to achieve in life. And it doesn't necessarily have to be aligned with a goal — even taking it one day at a time can be one of your goals. But the most important factor in it is this: what is your motivation level? Are you comfortable with your emotions, or not comfortable. How do you regulate your emotions? A great many emotions come into this, and in our society some emotions are seen as very negative. For instance, anxiety falls into that category. Aggression falls into it, anger falls into it. If these very things are regulated in a healthy way, they can actually be beneficial for you.

Attia Altaf02:30

You feel stressed about it. You get upset. You can't focus. And when that stress keeps building and building, and you can't deal with it on a day-to-day level, then it turns into anxiety. What is anxiety? Anxiety is fear. Anxiety is that you are kind of worried about everything. A fear settles inside you, and you can't work out whether you can deal with it or not. Attia Altaf03:44

And the biggest point I'd like to make is that mental illness is not only mental illness. You can't work out how to accept your emotions, how to navigate through them, how to come out of them. Attia Altaf12:17

Exactly, and the issue with it, as it seems to me, is that we go into these distractions to avoid our present moment. Learning to Recognise and Regulate Your Own Emotions

Attia Altaf14:14

Identifying it isn't that easy, because if a person had that acceptance — that this is my emotion and I can deal with it — then there'd be no problem at all. In fact, we start getting even angrier at them. So if a person is mentally stable, they will sit with their emotions. They won't find it that difficult to identify, because they can easily identify their healthy expressions and their unhealthy expressions — the ones they convey to other people, or to themselves, in the form of their emotions. So in a healthy way — suppose you've become angry — your anger is a kind of indication for you that something has happened which triggered you ...is. Your parents have said something, or a friend of yours has said something. That triggering point has arrived. You've identified it. Now you need to sit with it for a moment and not react to it. You have to tell yourself: right now I am feeling angry. How am I going to express this in a healthy way? If I direct it negatively at other people — if I shout, if I scream — that will create problems. Sit with yourself. Ask yourself how you want to say it. And you can tell people in a very calm and cool manner — if you just give yourself a little time. If you give an instant reaction, then things go very badly.

What University Students Are Actually Bringing to Therapy

Saad Zulfiqar Khan23:30

you are a professor as well — so is this found more among university-going students? What mental health issues are common? Which issues have you seen and addressed? Students must definitely bring things to you. What are the common issues, and what do you suggest to them?

Attia Altaf23:46

Look, it's been something like 18 years that I've been counselling students, and they come to me — they trust me. Apart from that, their counselling sessions — sometimes I do a proper session with them too. Otherwise, within the counselling hours, however many students come, I deal with them. From what I've seen so far, the biggest issue I've noticed is that parents don't have acceptance for their own child. That child has to make an effort to convince his parents that "I am your child" — and that becomes such a deep wound for him: "If I don't get 99% marks, then I'm not their child at all. They won't be proud of me. They won't take pride in me." The child is handed certain set goals — "Son, if you achieve this, then we will be proud of you, otherwise you're not our child." Their mental level is different. It's different. Now let me tell you the basic issues I have identified so far during the counselling sessions. First of all — I won't say that the joint family system is bad or anything like that. But within it, parents don't have the awareness that your children, with your relatives, with uncles and aunts, are not safe. Most of the abuse cases that have come to me so far — and I couldn't work out how to deal with them — it's within the family that a whole cycle of sexual abuse is running, and those children were the ones affected by it. Their parents will never believe it — that their grandfather could do such a thing to them. That their maternal grandfather could do this. That their uncle could do this. A whole personality pattern has already formed. I don't know when we as people will learn how to keep our children safe.

How to Support Someone Who Is Struggling

Saad Zulfiqar Khan25:44

We've seen it happen with these things, and especially shisha and that sort of thing is very common. So how can we — firstly, how can we identify that this person has now started going down this road? And if someone is at an early stage, can we ourselves counsel them, or bring them back from that?

Attia Altaf26:14

Exactly, absolutely they can. Someone who is already fighting this battle alone he simply can't work out where he stands. Where is he in society? What is his level? How is he supposed to deal with his emotions? He's in a struggling phase. Now if one more person comes and stands with him — even if they convey it silently. Of course they may not be able to put it into words, or give him professional help of that kind. I understand that. But someone simply being there and telling him, "I'm with you. Whatever you're going through, I understand it" — for him that will perhaps be worth more than the help he would get by going and seeking it professionally. If, even before that, he gets this little bit of support at the initial level, then that is more than enough. And the point is that it isn't necessarily that bad things are happening to you. The point is that good things aren't happening to you. So this can be a really valuable contribution — something you can do for your friends, for your cousins, for your younger brothers and sisters. And the most important thing is knowing how to go about it, because in our culture supporting someone tends to mean going up to them and saying, "You don't even pray. Your faith is so weak. That's why this is happening to you. Honestly, you've just got into the habit of making a drama out of everything." "You're an attention seeker." We start handing out labels like this. Then he starts questioning himself — where do I stand? And if I'm already going through this phase, do I now have to listen to all of this as well? So you mustn't compare him with anyone. Don't tell him his faith is weak. Simply sit with him and share a silence with someone in that kind of distress, someone who has already been through a very tough phase. That becomes an enormous thing.

What Needs to Change

Saad Zulfiqar Khan28:04

Quite right. Let's move towards the conclusion. Finally, how important do you think the role of the media is, and in what way can the media play its part in all of this?

Attia Altaf28:14

Look, the first thing is that we need to educate our parents. The background they come from — they had no awareness of any kind relating to mental health. They were never taught to regulate their emotions either. They were never taught acceptance either. They too had to fight very hard to win approval. Right? So when, from further back, there is what we call in simple words generational wounds — your parents are carrying those generational wounds too, and they're passing them on and transferring them into you. So the media can play this role: it can make programmes of this kind for parents and for children, and make them in a very light way. It shouldn't turn it into a bogeyman and present it as though this person is now about to go mad. In a much lighter way — through small programmes like this, through short public service messages, through reels — you can create awareness, and in a very sympathetic way. It must not be presented as a threat.
